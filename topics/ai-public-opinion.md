Public attitudes toward AI are characterized by a central tension: optimism about personal benefits coexists with anxiety about collective economic effects. Globally, the share of respondents who say AI offers more benefits than drawbacks rose from 55% in 2024 to 59% in 2025 — even as the share saying AI products make them nervous rose to 52%. These patterns are not contradictory: respondents consistently associate AI with personal time savings and practical benefits while remaining skeptical about its effects on labor markets and their country's economy.

## Regional Variation and the Optimism Gap

Southeast Asian countries are consistently the most optimistic about AI. In Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Singapore, more than 80% of respondents expect AI to profoundly change their lives in the next 3–5 years, and excitement consistently outpaces nervousness. India showed the sharpest increase in AI nervousness of any surveyed country between 2024 and 2025 (+14 percentage points), despite only a modest increase in excitement (+2). North American and European respondents cluster at lower excitement and higher nervousness relative to global averages, while China and Indonesia show the highest excitement levels.

Workplace AI adoption follows a similar geographic pattern. In 2025, 58% of employees globally reported using AI at work on a semiregular or regular basis (University of Melbourne/KPMG survey, 48,340 respondents across 47 countries). In India, China, Nigeria, the UAE, and Saudi Arabia, the share exceeded 80%. By contrast, roughly half of employees in North American and European countries reported regular use — and trust in AI for work purposes in those regions ran several points lower than usage rates, between 40–48%. Emerging economies thus represent both the highest current adoption and the highest institutional trust in AI.

## Expert-Public Divide

Across nearly every dimension surveyed, AI experts are substantially more optimistic than the US public. The largest gaps appear around employment: 73% of AI experts expect AI to have a positive impact on how people do their jobs, compared to 23% of US adults — a 50-point gap. Similar divides appear for the economy (69% vs 21%), K–12 education (61% vs 24%), and medical care (84% vs 44%). Both groups converge only on domains tied to trust and social connection — elections, personal relationships, and news — where optimism is low across both populations.

Experts also predict significantly faster AI adoption timelines. The median expert prediction is that generative AI will assist 18% of US work hours by 2030; the public forecasts 10%. Across 68 forecasts from the Longitudinal Expert AI Panel (LEAP survey), experts are 16% more likely than the public to predict faster AI progress; in capability-focused forecasts, the public's expectations diverge from experts' in 91% of cases, with the public expecting slower progress 71% of those times.

On job displacement, 64% of US adults expect AI to lead to fewer jobs over the next 20 years, compared to 39% of experts; only 5% of the public expect more jobs, versus 19% of experts. Both groups agree that cashiers, factory workers, and journalists face higher automation risk, while experts see greater risk for truck drivers and lawyers than the public does.

## Trust in AI Governance Institutions

The United States reported the lowest trust in its own government to regulate AI responsibly of any country surveyed at 31%, compared to the global average of 54%. Singapore (81%), Indonesia (76%), Malaysia (73%), and Thailand (70%) reported the highest government trust levels. Trust patterns are asymmetric across governing bodies as well: across Pew's 25-country survey, a median of 53% said they trust the EU to regulate AI effectively, compared to 37% for the United States and 27% for China — making the EU the most globally trusted AI regulator despite not leading in AI capability.

Across all 50 US states, concern about too little AI regulation outweighs concern about too much (41% vs 27%); more than one-third of respondents were unsure. Concern about insufficient regulation was strongest among older adults (65+, 51%) and college graduates (46%), and was not significantly differentiated by political affiliation (45% Democrat vs 40% Republican).

## AI Companion Attitudes

Global views on AI companionship are mixed. In a 2026 Ipsos-Google survey, 52% of worldwide respondents reported some level of excitement about using AI for companionship; the United States and Canada had the largest shares who were not excited at all (36% and 34% respectively). AI experts forecast 10% of US adults will use an AI companion daily by 2027, rising to 30% by 2040; the public forecasts considerably lower adoption at 20% by 2040.

## Worker Perceptions and AI Optimism

The April 2026 Anthropic Economic Index Survey, linked to usage data from approximately 9,700 Claude users, provides the first systematic view of how Claude usage patterns correlate with worker attitudes. The survey sample skews heavily toward computer and mathematical occupations (30%) and management (23%) and is not representative of the general workforce, but its linkage to actual usage data enables findings not available from general-population surveys.

Close to 60% of respondents expect to handle a larger share of their work with AI in 12 months than today, and over 35% expect AI to handle most or nearly all of their work tasks within the year. Anticipated pace of AI capability growth is strikingly uniform across occupation types — workers in high AI-exposure roles expect roughly the same increment of future AI capability as those in low-exposure roles, regardless of their current usage level or country GDP. This "rising tide" pattern holds even though reported current exposure is 10 percentage points lower in high-GDP countries and 10 percentage points lower among workers with 15 or more years of experience.

Counter to displacement-anxiety predictions, workers who delegate the most to Claude are the most optimistic. Across all six measured dimensions of job quality — pay, job security, job-finding ability, meaning, autonomy, and human interaction — higher automation share correlates with more positive expectations for AI's impact. The relationship holds when controlling for user tenure. Respondents are notably more worried about junior colleagues than themselves: over one third estimate more than a 60% probability that a junior colleague will lose a job in the next year, while only 10% rate their own job loss as likely or very likely — slightly below the 13.4% US annual discharge baseline.

Gender differences in Claude usage are pronounced: women's Claude Code session share is 6.3 percentage points lower and automation share is 7.3 percentage points lower than men's, controlling for occupation, while women log more active chat time — indicating more iterative, collaborative engagement patterns rather than full task delegation.

## Teaching Notes

**Concept in plain terms.** Global surveys show AI optimism and nervousness rising simultaneously, with a systematic expert-public gap: 73% of AI experts expect positive job impact vs. 23% of the US public. Among actual Claude users, those who delegate most to AI are the most optimistic about AI's effects on their jobs — counter to the assumption that heavy automation exposure produces anxiety.

**Why it matters for instruction.** The expert-public gap shapes the receptivity of audiences to AI training and the feasibility of governance conversations. The Anthropic survey adds a counterpoint within active users: heavier AI delegation correlates with confidence, not concern. Instructors must distinguish the general-public anxiety pattern (awareness without use tends to produce nervousness) from the active-user optimism pattern (use correlates with optimism).

**Common misconceptions.** High AI awareness in general populations correlates with nervousness about labor market effects, especially in North America — but high AI use among actual users correlates with the opposite. Both patterns coexist. The audience's level of active AI use determines which is most relevant.

**Suggested framing.** Start with the expert-public gap as a calibration problem: your audience's starting assumptions about AI's near-term employment impact will diverge significantly from expert consensus. Then use the automation-optimism finding to explore whether direct AI experience changes those assumptions differently than awareness alone does.
