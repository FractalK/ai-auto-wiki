This article reports on Stanford HAI's AI+Science: Accelerating Discovery conference (May 5, 2026), where researchers across neuroscience, climate science, biology, physics, and mathematics examined AI's evolving role in scientific practice. Key demonstrations include the Samudra ocean emulator (1,000 years of climate simulated per day on a single GPU, versus 12 years per day for traditional models), James Zou's Virtual Lab — autonomous AI agents that designed COVID variant antibody binders within days and were experimentally confirmed to outperform previous human-designed nanobodies — and rapid AI mathematical reasoning progress (IMO silver medal in July 2024 to a perfect score on the hardest undergraduate mathematics exam by December 2025). The conference's central theme is that AI excels at deductive science — pattern-filling within known domains — while human judgment remains essential for abductive science, problem selection, and community vetting of results. Structural risks highlighted include skill atrophy in scientific training pipelines, a monoculture effect in which AI-assisted papers receive approximately 300% more citations but converge on the same datasets and questions, and a peer review system already strained by volume that AI-generated research could push past functional viability.
