An OpenAI general-purpose reasoning model autonomously disproved the Erdős planar unit distance conjecture — a prominent open problem in combinatorial geometry first posed in 1946 — constructing an infinite family of n-point configurations with at least n^{1+δ} unit-distance pairs for a fixed exponent δ > 0, disproving the decades-long consensus that square grid constructions were essentially optimal. The proof applied tools from algebraic number theory — specifically infinite class field towers and Golod–Shafarevich theory — to an elementary geometric question, drawing on mathematical connections that domain experts had not previously recognized. External verification was provided by Fields medalist Tim Gowers, leading number theorists Arul Shankar and Jacob Tsimerman, and a subsequent refinement by Princeton's Will Sawin established an explicit lower bound of δ = 0.014. OpenAI characterizes the result as the first instance of an AI autonomously resolving a prominent open problem central to an active mathematical subfield, and notes that the model was a general-purpose reasoner — not a system specifically trained for mathematics or scaffolded for proof search. Pass@1 accuracy on this problem scaled from 0% to 48% across a log scale of test-time compute from 1× to 16×, illustrating the role of extended inference in enabling the breakthrough.
